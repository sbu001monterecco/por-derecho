## Editorial rule

This is a narrative manuscript, not a pleading and not an evidential register. It must distinguish documented acts, the author's allegations, reasonable investigative questions, professional opinions and unresolved matters. It must not present collusion, criminal intent, misuse of confidential information, procedural fraud, improper remate destination or professional misconduct as established facts unless and until the relevant evidence or competent decision supports that proposition.

The manuscript should remain capable of changing when Cuatrecasas, the courts, professional bodies or other participants provide documentary explanations that narrow, rebut or resolve the questions described here.

# PROLOGUE — WHEN YOUR LAWYER BECOMES YOUR CREDITOR

The simplest version of this story sounds almost ordinary.

A client hires a law firm. The law firm does work. The bills are not fully paid. The law firm eventually sues.

There is nothing inherently scandalous in that sequence.

But Sun Park was never a simple sequence.

By the time Cuatrecasas became a creditor pursuing Matkator, the firm had already spent years inside the economic, legal and strategic perimeter of the hotel. It had known the companies. It had known the insolvency. It had known the financing problem. It had known the role of Matkator. It had known the pressure around Construcciones Acosta Matos. It had known that the continued existence of the hotel as an accessible, financeable, operational asset mattered to any rescue.

Then came a reversal that deserves its own book.

The adviser became the creditor.

The entity that had once formed part of the protected perimeter became the execution debtor.

And property inside the same hotel perimeter became the object of adjudication and possible cession of remate.

The question is not whether lawyers may recover unpaid fees. They may.

The question is what professional history travels with them when they do.

That is the story of the four green houses and the one red hotel.

The title is a provocation, but not a verdict. The four houses are a play on the name Cuatrecasas. The green is the author's judgment on advice that, at critical moments, he came to regard as insufficiently protective of a hotel already flashing red. The red hotel is Sun Park: one physical asset moving through insolvency, disputed control, attempted rescue, adjudication, transformation and later operation under another name.

This book asks whether a professional mandate can be understood file by file when the economic object being protected was one continuous place.

# PART I — THE CLIENT DID NOT HIRE A FILE

## Chapter 1 — The Perimeter

Cuatrecasas did not enter the story as an outside debt collector with no knowledge of the asset.

The surviving record places the firm inside a wider professional relationship concerning Aweswell, Luchy Playa Blanca, Matkator and Sun Park. By 2019, financing correspondence described a rescue architecture involving Aweswell as borrower, LPB and Matkator as property-holding entities, CAM as an adverse commercial actor, security over assets, exploitation rights, an exit from liquidation and possible refinancing or sale.

That matters because the first dispute in this book is not about negligence. It is about the object of the mandate.

Was Cuatrecasas retained for isolated legal tasks, each capable of being judged separately? Or did the work, taken as a whole, require the firm to understand and protect an integrated economic rescue?

The answer cannot responsibly be invented from hindsight. It belongs in the engagement letters, matter-opening records, invoices, time entries, instructions, financing papers and communications.

But the evidence already makes one thing difficult to deny: the firm understood that Sun Park was not merely a collection of unrelated files.

The hotel, the companies, the financing, the insolvency and the ability to preserve value were connected.

That is the first house.

## Chapter 2 — What Was Being Protected?

A hotel rescue is not secured only by a mortgage deed or a court order.

It depends on practical conditions.

Can a lender inspect the asset? Can a valuer enter it? Are the rooms and common areas in the condition represented? Who controls the keys? Who can operate the hotel? Who can grant or document the security? Who can show a funder that the business can continue after money is advanced?

The Sun Park rescue problem therefore had two layers.

There was the legal layer: insolvency, creditor ranking, appeals, security, title and authority.

And there was the physical-economic layer: possession, access, condition, operation, income and confidence.

The central contention developed by Gil Marer and Aweswell is that those layers were not adequately protected as one system.

Cuatrecasas may answer that it was never retained to perform every function later attributed to it. That defence must be taken seriously. The book should test the actual mandate rather than create one retrospectively.

But if the mandate was as integrated as the financing work suggests, the adequacy of the advice cannot be judged solely by asking whether each individual filing was competent.

The question becomes whether the work protected the economic object the lawyers themselves understood.

# PART II — JUNE 2018

## Chapter 3 — The Red Alert

The critical factual turning point is June 2018.

According to contemporaneous communications from the Aweswell side, CAM / Acosta Matos had entered the Sun Park complex in circumstances described not as ordinary co-owner access but as an anticipatory takeover. The reports referred to security, chains, lock changes, disputed Community authority, access problems and alleged entry into areas regarded as belonging to Luchy.

Those communications do not prove that every allegation was legally or factually correct.

They prove something narrower and highly relevant: the lawyers were told that the client believed a physical-control event was taking place at the hotel.

The professional response was not absolute silence. That distinction matters.

There were discussions about information from the Community. Matkator was considered as an owner capable of requesting information. The insolvency administrator was discussed. The Arrecife route and possible precautionary measures were live. There were appeal and suspension efforts. Later in 2018, a formal Registry intervention challenged a CAM acquisition route involving LPB assets.

The more credible criticism is therefore not that nobody did anything.

It is that the response may have been fragmented when the threat was unitary.

Access was one issue.

Community authority was another.

The insolvency administrator was another.

The Registry was another.

Arrecife was another.

The Mercantile Court was another.

But the hotel was one.

## Chapter 4 — The Decision to Wait

The surviving June correspondence suggests that the problem was recognised as potentially serious enough to have criminal or precautionary implications and serious enough to raise the question whether the Mercantile Court should be informed.

At the same time, the group was trying to preserve a possible route to conclude the insolvency by payment and financing.

This is where the book must resist easy hindsight.

A lawyer in June 2018 had to choose among imperfect options, under time pressure, inside a contentious insolvency, with questions of standing and with the insolvency administrator occupying a central legal role.

Waiting for stronger Arrecife measures while preserving a possible consensual or funded insolvency exit may have seemed strategically rational.

The question is whether it remained rational once the alleged physical-control event, disputed Community structure, difficulties obtaining information and risks to value were considered together.

The strongest version of the professional-failure thesis is not: "they ignored us."

It is:

> They saw multiple components of the danger, but may not have converted them into one emergency asset-protection programme.

That question should be answered by the missing internal and external record: what urgent steps were considered, what were rejected, what were filed, what were deferred, what evidence was preserved and what was handed to the next lawyers.

# PART III — WHEN PARTIAL ACTION PROVES KNOWLEDGE

## Chapter 5 — December 2018

One of the most important documents in the story is also one of the most inconvenient for a simplistic accusation.

In December 2018, Cuatrecasas lawyer Rosa Gual took a formal position before the Property Registry concerning a CAM transaction involving LPB assets. The position, as preserved in the record, treated the transaction as potentially inconsistent with the liquidation plan, judicial suspension and ob rem links and sought resistance to registration or cancellation if registration had occurred.

That intervention shows action.

It also shows knowledge.

Cuatrecasas was not treating CAM as an ordinary, neutral market participant whose dealings with Sun Park assets required no scrutiny.

The document therefore cuts both ways.

It helps Cuatrecasas rebut any allegation of total indifference.

It helps the author ask a harder question: if the firm understood that asset transfers to CAM could require urgent formal resistance, why was the same level of integrated protection not carried through — or was it — across possession, Community legitimacy, asset condition, the insolvency administrator, financing, restitution and damage preservation?

A fair book cannot answer that before the complete file is obtained.

But a fair book can insist that the answer matters.

## Chapter 6 — A Window to Save the Complex

By 2019, the professional relationship had moved into financing and rescue architecture.

The record includes correspondence in which Cuatrecasas professionals described favourable developments and the possibility that the complex might still be saved, while also making clear that the firm could not continue indefinitely without payment.

This is one of the moral and professional tensions at the centre of the book.

A law firm is a business. Lawyers are not required to finance a client's litigation indefinitely. Unpaid fees matter. A firm can withdraw.

But withdrawal at a critical moment raises a different question from entitlement to payment.

What must be preserved when a lawyer leaves?

What must the next lawyer be told?

Which deadlines, risks, unresolved legal questions, factual baselines and strategic warnings must cross the handover intact?

The issue is not whether Cuatrecasas was entitled to stop working.

The issue is whether the rescue knowledge survived the exit.

# PART IV — THE ECONOMIC GATE

## Chapter 7 — A Number Can Change Meaning

The Sun Park story contains a technical problem with enormous practical consequences.

A figure can appear in one procedural context for one purpose and later acquire the appearance of being a definitive economic truth.

The current Control 24 / AP4 analysis asks whether an amount connected to a third-party improvement threshold later became treated as if it were a firm, operative premise in the adjudication economics.

The manuscript must be careful here. Cuatrecasas did not write the court's decisions, and the possible responsibility of judicial actors is a separate question.

The professional question is different.

If rescue counsel knew that survival depended on knowing exactly what had to be paid, guaranteed or outbid, did the lawyers isolate the difference between recognised credit, mortgage liability, ordinary interest, default interest, a third-party threshold, CAM's credit position, cash required from a third party and the final economic result for the estate?

A competent answer may exist in files not yet obtained.

If it does, the allegation narrows.

If it does not, the omission matters because a rescue can fail simply because nobody can produce a reliable number for the money required to close it.

## Chapter 8 — The Handover Test

A proper handover in a matter like Sun Park would not merely transfer folders.

It would transfer danger.

It would tell the next lawyers what could not be allowed to disappear into procedural history: the physical-control event; Community authority questions; the Registry problem; disputed interest and threshold issues; the difference between CAM credit and third-party cash; the status of financing; the role of Matkator; the next procedural windows; and the consequences of delay.

Whether Cuatrecasas did this fully is unresolved.

That unresolved question is stronger than a premature finding.

The handover file is one of the documents that can materially change the book.

# PART V — THE INVERSION

## Chapter 9 — Matkator

Matkator is the hinge.

It appears on both sides of the story.

On the asset side, Matkator was a property-holding company inside the Sun Park perimeter and was identified in 2018 as an owner through which information and rights concerning the Community might be pursued.

On the later debt side, Matkator became the entity against which Cuatrecasas pursued its fee recovery.

This does not establish that Matkator was not liable.

The pagarés may have created enforceable obligations. The engagement and billing material may provide a clean explanation. A court may have recognised the claim on a valid basis.

But formal enforceability does not answer every professional question.

The required reconstruction is simple enough to state:

**Who was the client? Who instructed the work? Who benefited? Who was invoiced? Who was expected to pay? Why did Matkator issue the pagarés? Were they payment, security, guarantee or settlement instruments? Why was Matkator selected for enforcement? Which asset was selected, and why?**

That chain is not yet complete.

Until it is, neither side should be allowed to simplify the story beyond the documents.

## Chapter 10 — The Adviser Becomes the Executant

The later enforcement conduct is the moment the narrative changes genre.

According to the existing ETJ and professional-conduct materials, Cuatrecasas did not merely hold an old unpaid claim. In 2024–2026 it took affirmative steps in the enforcement route: seeking adjudication, resisting suspension or further expert verification, addressing allegations that the asset had been physically transformed or integrated into the hotel operation, and reserving the possibility of ceding the remate to a third party.

The complete certified ETJ and Cambiario records are still essential. The book should not overstate a summary where the full judicial file can provide the exact words and procedural effect.

But the narrative significance is already obvious.

The former adviser was now using the coercive machinery of enforcement against a company inside the same economic perimeter it had previously helped to structure and protect.

The question becomes one of professional continuity:

What prior knowledge remained relevant?

What conflict analysis was performed?

What asset information informed the choice of enforcement target?

What did the firm know about the physical status and use of the Matkator property?

And who, if anyone, was contemplated as a possible ultimate cessionary or beneficiary of the remate?

## Chapter 11 — The Most Important Unknown Person

The most important person in this part of the book may be a person who does not exist.

The possible cessionary.

A reservation of the right to cede a remate can be ordinary procedural language. It does not prove that a third party was waiting in the wings.

If no cessionary was ever contemplated, the record should be capable of establishing that.

If one was contemplated, its identity becomes material.

Not because every third party is suspicious, but because the identity could radically change the meaning of the enforcement.

An unrelated buyer would support one interpretation.

A person connected to CAM, HNT, MYND Yaiza, Canarian Hospitality, RIC Private Equity or the Acosta Matos environment would create a very different investigative question.

At present, the manuscript must not claim such a connection.

The honest line is more powerful:

> The beneficiary of any contemplated cession must be identified — or its absence certified — before the story can be closed.

# PART VI — KNOWLEDGE AFTER THE MANDATE

## Chapter 12 — March 2021

Years after the most critical 2018 events, Cuatrecasas / Íñigo de Luisa received renewed information from Gil concerning Sun Park, RIC/CNMV and the Acosta Matos environment.

The current record supports an early notice point in March 2021, followed by a WhatsApp/audio exchange.

It does not prove misuse of confidential information.

This distinction must remain explicit.

The legitimate investigative question is what happened to the information: was it preserved, discussed, shared, connected to others, or simply received and left dormant?

The book should never turn receipt into misuse by implication.

But neither should it treat later enforcement as if the former advisers knew nothing about the continuing controversy surrounding the same asset.

Knowledge has a chronology.

That chronology matters.

# PART VII — FOUR FRONTS

## Chapter 13 — Enforcement

The first front is ETJ 163/2020 and the underlying Cambiario 1048/2019.

This is the hardest current conduct because it can be read directly from court acts once the certified file is complete.

The task is to reconstruct every affirmative Cuatrecasas step from 2019 through 2026, distinguish routine enforcement from new discretionary acts and identify whether adjudication or cession remained live.

## Chapter 14 — Civil Accountability

The second front is civil and pre-action accountability.

The emerging strategy is not to begin with one giant negligence accusation. It is to obtain the documents that define the real mandate and the real debt: engagement letters, invoices, matter codes, time entries, payment records, pagaré communications, the recobro transition, conflict checks, handover material and any remate/cession documentation.

The book should follow the same discipline.

Disclosure before accusation.

## Chapter 15 — Professional Conduct

The third front is the professional-conduct system.

Complaints, preliminary files, appeals and later submissions concerning Cuatrecasas and individual lawyers must be described by exact procedural status.

A complaint is not a finding.

An appeal from an archive is not proof that the archive was wrong.

The value of the deontological record may lie as much in Cuatrecasas's own explanations as in the disciplinary outcome. A current account given to a professional body can be compared with contemporaneous emails and with court filings.

Where the positions reconcile, the criticism should narrow.

Where they do not, the inconsistency becomes a question requiring explanation.

## Chapter 16 — DP 748/2026

The fourth front is criminal-procedural, and it requires the greatest restraint.

The purpose should not be to convert an unpaid-fee dispute into a criminal accusation.

The legitimate inquiry is narrower: whether the execution operated on materially incomplete appearances concerning asset identity, debtor/cause, prior knowledge, court representations, economic prejudice and possible remate beneficiary.

The book must not state that Cuatrecasas committed estafa procesal unless a competent process establishes it.

The investigative proposition is sufficient:

> Was this ordinary fee recovery, or did the enforcement risk becoming a procedural bridge through which a residual asset position in Sun Park could be transferred or consolidated in a manner connected to the wider displacement history?

# PART VIII — THE DEFENCE

## Chapter 17 — The Case for Cuatrecasas

A book that cannot state the best defence is not ready for publication.

Cuatrecasas's strongest possible account is substantial.

The firm did real work. It took protective steps. It pursued appeals and suspension. It intervened at the Registry. It worked on financing and rescue. It achieved or contributed to favourable results. It was entitled to expect payment. It was not obliged to finance the litigation indefinitely. It may have had a narrower retainer than the client later came to believe. Matkator may have issued valid pagarés. Later enforcement may have been wholly lawful and independent of the earlier mandate. Reservation of cession may have been standard procedural language. No evidence presently proves that an Acosta Matos-linked party was a remate beneficiary. No evidence presently proves that confidential information was misused. And later events cannot automatically make a contemporaneously reasonable legal strategy negligent.

Every serious chapter must survive that defence.

If the evidence cannot answer it, the manuscript should say so.

## Chapter 18 — The Causation Problem

Even a proven professional failure does not automatically equal the value of the hotel.

The most difficult question is causation.

Would an urgent filing in June 2018 have been admitted?

Would it have changed possession or access?

Would a preserved physical baseline have altered later litigation?

Was rescue financing genuinely available?

Would the insolvency court or administrator have acted differently?

Could CAM have reached substantially the same outcome through another lawful route?

Did the handover materially reduce the probability of rescue?

Did any AP4/interest modelling failure actually cause a funder to walk away?

These are counterfactual questions, not rhetorical ones.

The book should not calculate damages by subtracting the present from an imagined perfect past.

It should model lost chances, one decision point at a time.

# PART IX — ONE RED HOTEL

## Chapter 19 — What Professional Advice Costs

The invoices are the visible cost of lawyers.

The invisible cost is opportunity.

Good professional advice preserves choices.

It preserves evidence before evidence disappears.

It preserves deadlines before they close.

It preserves the ability to finance before uncertainty becomes unfinanceable.

It preserves the distinction between a disputed number and a final number.

It preserves the next lawyer's ability to understand the danger without rereading years of history.

And, when a former adviser becomes a creditor, it preserves confidence that prior knowledge has not been converted into an unfair advantage.

The central accusation of this book is therefore deliberately conditional.

Not that Cuatrecasas caused every loss.

Not that Cuatrecasas joined an unlawful scheme.

Not that every strategic decision was wrong.

But that the full professional trajectory must be reconstructed before the later enforcement can be treated as an ordinary fee dispute detached from the hotel it grew out of.

## Chapter 20 — What Would Change My Mind

A credible investigative book should say what evidence would weaken its own thesis.

The following would materially narrow the case:

- engagement documents showing narrow, clearly delimited retainers inconsistent with an integrated rescue duty;
- a complete June 2018 emergency strategy file demonstrating proportionate advice, evidence preservation and court action;
- a full handover showing that every critical risk was clearly transferred to successor counsel;
- a clean invoice/pagaré ledger establishing Matkator as the appropriate direct debtor for the relevant services;
- contemporaneous conflict reviews explaining and approving the transition to recobro and enforcement;
- a certified ETJ record showing that cession language was purely generic and that no third-party beneficiary was contemplated;
- records demonstrating that the firm had no relevant improper contact with the CAM/HNT/MYND/RIC/Acosta Matos perimeter concerning the Matkator enforcement;
- a coherent AP4/interest analysis showing that the economic-gate issues were identified, preserved and handed over.

The opposite evidence would strengthen the thesis.

That is the difference between investigation and advocacy.

# EPILOGUE — THE QUESTION THAT REMAINS

A law firm is not responsible for the whole world surrounding a client.

A lawyer cannot guarantee a rescue.

A court can reject good arguments. A client can run out of money. A commercial plan can fail. A creditor can enforce. A company can lose an asset without anyone committing professional misconduct.

But none of those propositions resolves the Sun Park question.

The same firm that knew the rescue perimeter later pursued one of its residual property holders.

The same asset appears on both sides of that professional history.

That is why the book cannot end with the phrase "unpaid fees."

It must end with a demand for the chain.

Client.

Instructor.

Beneficiary.

Invoice.

Payer.

Pagaré.

Debtor.

Asset.

Adjudication.

Cessionary.

Final beneficiary.

Show the chain, and the story may become simpler.

Until then, four green houses stand around one red hotel.
